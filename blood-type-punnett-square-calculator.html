Blood type Punnett square calculator

Pick both parents' blood types and get every possible child type with the odds. A blood type does not pin down a genotype, so this works through every genotype the parents could have.

The squares behind it

Type A can be AA or AO, and type B can be BB or BO, so more than one square may apply. Each is shown with the odds it produces.

Why a blood type is not a genotype

ABO blood type runs on three alleles, not the usual two: A, B and O. A and B are codominant, so someone carrying both shows both antigens and is type AB. O is recessive to both.

That means type A blood is either AA or AO, and a blood test cannot tell you which. The same goes for type B. Only AB and O are unambiguous: AB must be AB, and O must be OO. This is exactly why two type A parents can produce a type O child, and why that result is not evidence of anything except recessive alleles doing what recessive alleles do.

The one cross worth memorising. An AB parent and an O parent have children who are type A or type B, never AB or O. AB passes A or B, O passes O, so every child is AO or BO. It shows up on exams constantly. The one real exception is cis-AB, described below.

Rh factor is a separate gene

Rh inheritance is ordinary dominance and has nothing to do with the ABO gene. Rh positive is DD or Dd, Rh negative is dd. Two Rh positive parents who both carry a hidden d have a 1 in 4 chance of an Rh negative child. Two Rh negative parents can only have Rh negative children, since neither has a D to pass on.

Because the two genes are independent, the calculator above multiplies the ABO odds by the Rh odds when you set both.

This cannot establish paternity. A Punnett square can sometimes exclude a parent, if a child's blood type is impossible from that cross. It can never confirm one, because millions of people share any given blood type. Real testing uses DNA markers.

Common questions

Can two parents with type A blood have a type O child?

Yes. Type A blood can be genotype AA or AO. If both parents are AO, each can pass the O allele, so there is a 1 in 4 chance of an OO child, who is type O. This surprises people because neither parent is type O, but the O allele is recessive and can be carried silently for generations.

Can an AB parent and an O parent have an AB or O child?

Almost never, and for the standard alleles never at all. An AB parent passes either A or B, an O parent is OO and always passes O, so every child is AO or BO, meaning type A or type B. The exception is cis-AB, a rare allele carrying both the A and B antigens together, which leaves the other allele free to be O. A cis-AB parent crossed with an O parent can therefore have a type O child. Cis-AB is uncommon and is seen most often in Korean and Japanese families.

What blood types can a type A and a type B parent have?

All four. If both parents are heterozygous (AO and BO), the children can be AB, A, B or O, each with a 1 in 4 chance. It is the only phenotype pairing that can produce every blood type.

Why can a blood type not tell you the genotype?

Because A and B are dominant over O. A person with type A blood is either AA or AO, and there is no way to tell from a blood test alone, only from family history or genetic testing. Type AB and type O are the exceptions: AB must be AB, and O must be OO.

How is Rh factor inherited?

Separately from ABO, and as a simple dominant trait. Rh positive is DD or Dd, Rh negative is dd. Two Rh positive parents who are both Dd have a 1 in 4 chance of an Rh negative child. Two Rh negative parents can only have Rh negative children.

Can a blood type Punnett square prove paternity?

It can sometimes exclude, but it can never confirm. If a cross makes a child's blood type impossible, that rules the parent out. But millions of people share any given blood type, so a compatible result proves nothing. Actual paternity testing uses DNA markers, not blood type.

Why is ABO inheritance called codominance?

Because when someone inherits both A and B, neither masks the other and both antigens appear on the red blood cells, giving type AB. That is different from incomplete dominance, where you would get a blend. ABO also involves three alleles rather than the usual two, which is why it is a standard example of multiple allelism.

The cis-AB exception

Everything above assumes the ordinary arrangement, where an AB person carries an A allele on one chromosome and a B allele on the other. That is called trans-AB, and for those people the rules hold exactly.

Cis-AB is different. A single allele encodes a transferase that builds both the A and the B antigen, so both sit together on one chromosome. That leaves the other chromosome free to carry an O. Written out, the genotype is cisAB/O rather than A/B.

The consequence is the one exception to the table above. A cis-AB parent crossed with an O parent can have a type O child, because the cis-AB parent has a real O allele to pass on. It also means a cis-AB parent and an O parent can have an AB child, since the whole cisAB unit travels together.

Cis-AB is rare and is reported most often in Korean and Japanese populations. It is worth knowing precisely because the standard rule is taught as absolute, and a family that runs into it can be told something about themselves that is not true. This section exists because a reader on r/genetics who is the O child of a cis-AB father pointed out that the chart was overstating the rule.
