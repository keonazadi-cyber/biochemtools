How antibody dilutions work

A dilution written as 1:500 means one part antibody in 500 parts total, not one part in 500 parts of diluent. So the antibody volume is simply the final volume divided by the dilution factor, and everything else is buffer. For 5 mL at 1:500 that is 5000 µL / 500 = 10 µL of antibody plus 4990 µL of diluent.

When a datasheet gives a working concentration instead of a ratio, convert first: divide the stock concentration by the working concentration, in matching units. A 1 mg/mL stock is 1000 µg/mL, so reaching 2 µg/mL needs 1000/2 = a 1:500 dilution. From there it is the same calculation.

Why very high dilutions need two steps

A 1:5000 dilution in 200 µL needs 0.04 µL of antibody. No pipette measures that, and even 1–2 µL carries a large percentage error on most pipettes. The fix is to split the factor across two steps, because dilution factors multiply:

1:100 then 1:50 = 100 × 50 = 1:5000 step 1 1 µL antibody + 99 µL diluent → 100 µL of 1:100 step 2 4 µL of that + 196 µL diluent → 200 µL of 1:5000

Both volumes are now comfortably pipettable, and the final concentration is identical. The calculator above flags this automatically and picks a sensible split whenever the direct volume falls below what you can measure.

FAQ

How do I make a 1:1000 dilution?
Final volume ÷ 1000 is the antibody volume. For 1 mL: 1 µL antibody + 999 µL buffer.

Does 1:500 include the antibody?
Yes, it means 1 part in 500 total, so 1 part antibody + 499 parts diluent.

How do I turn a working concentration into a ratio?
Divide stock by working concentration in the same units. 1 mg/mL (1000 µg/mL) to 2 µg/mL = 1:500.

What if the volume is too small to pipette?
Split it into two steps whose factors multiply to the total, for example 1:100 then 1:50 for 1:5000.

Which dilution should I start at?
Follow the datasheet, then titrate around it. The table above gives rough ranges per application.
